# Problem Framing and Stakeholders

## Domain: Turning Discovered Activities Into Real Plans

Young adults encounter things they'd love to do constantly on social media. A trendy restaurant appears in an Instagram Reel. TikTok recommends a new pottery studio. A friend sends a concert, workout class, jazz bar, volunteer event, neighborhood festival, any activity you could possibly think of.

These discoveries often happen at inconvenient moments: while waiting for class, lying in bed, commuting, or taking a short break between other obligations. The person is interested enough to think, *I would actually do that*, but not free enough to organize it immediately.

The current digital environment is unusually good at producing these moments of interest. Social feeds continuously surface restaurants, events, hobbies, and local experiences. The difficult transition happens afterward.

Actually doing the activity may require remembering it later, finding the original post again, deciding whom to ask, determining whether those people are genuinely interested, comparing schedules, and eventually making a commitment.

For relatively casual activities that amount of organization is often disproportionate to the original intention.

The problem is therefore not simply activity discovery or scheduling. It is the gap between:

> **“I would do that.”**

and

> **“We are doing that.”**

This project focuses on preserving the weak but genuine intention that currently disappears between those two states.

## Bad Situations

### 1. The intention disappears at the moment it is created

Josie is scrolling TikTok between classes when she sees a video about Wally's Cafe, a jazz club in Boston. She immediately thinks of her friend Marie.

Normally she sends the TikTok to Marie with:

> we should totally go here

Marie hearts the message and replies:

> wait yes

Both responses are completely genuine. But then Josie's class begins. Marie is in between lab experiments. Neither person wants to stop what she's doing and compare calendars.

The conversation continues later that day: "when are you getting home?", "how was class?", "can you help me pick an outfit?". By Friday the TikTok is buried underneath other messages. Nobody rejected the idea. Nobody changed her mind about the jazz club.

The intention simply had nowhere to persist.

This happens frequently in my own life. I've sent friends countless TikToks, posts, and reels about restaurants, workout classes, art classes, and community events that we genuinely wanted to try. But instead end up repeatedly saying “we should go” without ever doing so.

The existing workflow asks too much at exactly the wrong moment.

When someone has just discovered something interesting they may be willing to express interest with one tap. They are much less likely to stop scrolling, open another application, recreate the activity, select friends, find dates, and start organizing it.

### 2. Group-chat enthusiasm is ambiguous

Suppose Josie sends the jazz-bar TikTok into a group chat.

Marie hearts it. Anna responds “cute.” Emma sends a fire emoji.

Which of them would actually go?

The conversation doesn't encode that distinction.

A reaction might mean:

- I saw this.
- This looks fun.
- I like jazz.
- I would actually attend.
- I would attend if somebody else organized it.

For small activities, asking every person to clarify their commitment creates more friction than the activity feels worth.

As a result, Josie knows that her friends *liked the idea* but not whether there is enough shared willingness to act on it.

### 3. Free time arrives after the ideas disappear

On Saturday afternoon, Josie and Marie unexpectedly discover that they are both free.

They want to do something different.

Unfortunately the moment when they have time is not the moment when they originally discovered interesting things.

Josie remembers seeing “some jazz place” on TikTok but can't remember its name. Marie searches her saved Instagram posts. They scroll through old messages and suggest a few possibilities.

After enough searching and negotiation, planning itself starts to consume their free time.

They end up at the same restaurant they visit regularly.

The familiar activity wins, not necessarily because they prefer it, but because it has almost no activation cost.

Their problem isn'g a shortage of ideas: it is that the intentions they accumulated during the week are inaccessible when those intentions finally become actionable.

### 4. “I'd go if someone went with me”

A student sees a beginner boxing class and thinks it looks fun.

She would happily go with someone else but she's too nervous to go alone. 

Her closest friend isn't interested. 

She technically could individually message ten other friends but that feels excessive for an activity she is only tentatively curious about. Posting broadly—

> Does anyone want to take a boxing class with me?

—also makes a weak interest feel more public and committed than it really is.

So she does nothing.

Meanwhile an acquaintance or friend of a friend may have independently saved the same class while thinking exactly the same thing.

Each person's willingness exists privately. What is missing is a low-cost mechanism for allowing compatible intentions to encounter one another.

## Corroboration

Evidence about young people's relationship with social media supports a more nuanced version of this problem than simply claiming that phones are harmful.

Pew Research Center reported in 2025 that 45% of U.S. teenagers said they spend too much time on social media while 44% said they had tried to cut back on social media use. At the same time 74% said social media helps them feel more connected to their friends.

This tension matters for WeShould. TikTok and Instagram can simultaneously be a source of dissatisfaction with how free time is spent and a valuable source of social connection and discovery. It would be very difficult to convince a significant percentage of young adults to migrate off social media completely; however, if we instead used it as a tool to facilitate in-person interaction, we could alleviate some of this dissatisfaction. In the bad situations above, these platforms are often where users first encounter the restaurants, classes, concerts, and activities they want to experience offline.

The design therefore shouldn't require users to abandon the platforms where discovery already works. 

Instead, WeShould intervenes at the boundary between discovery and action.

- Pew Research Center, *Teens, Social Media and Mental Health* (2025):  
  https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/22/teens-social-media-and-mental-health/

The surveyed population is younger than the primary users of this project so the result should not be treated as direct evidence about MIT students. It does, however, corroborate the broader tension visible in the bad situations: digital platforms can simultaneously create connection and dissatisfaction with how people spend their free time.

The existence of services that reduce the friction of arranging offline social experiences also suggests that coordination itself is a meaningful cost. Timeleft, for example, organizes dinners and other real-world social experiences for users so that they do not have to assemble the group and logistics themselves.

- Timeleft, *What is Timeleft and How Does It Work?*:  
  https://help.timeleft.com/hc/en-150/articles/28529250732444-What-is-Timeleft-and-How-Does-It-Work

The evidence therefore does not support a simple claim that “phones are bad.” A narrower interpretation is more defensible:

> People frequently value offline social activity while the mechanics required to translate casual interest into coordinated action remain disproportionately expensive.

## Workarounds and Comparables

### TikTok and Instagram saves

Saving a post preserves the content but not the intention behind the save.

Users save things for many reasons: inspiration, entertainment, information, or simple curiosity.

Looking back six weeks later, a saved TikTok cannot distinguish:

> “I liked this video.”

from:

> “I genuinely want to go here.”

It also cannot reveal that two friends independently saved equivalent activities, since saves are private. 

### Group chats

Though messaging is useful once people are actively organizing something, it is poor storage for weak intentions.

Messages are chronological, reactions are ambiguous, and ideas disappear as conversation continues.

Someone must eventually remember the idea and volunteer to coordinate it.

### Notes, screenshots, and lists

Some people maintain lists of restaurants or activities.

These solve personal memory better than group chats but still isolate information by person.

A list can answer:

> Where did I want to go?

It cannot easily answer:

> Which of these places would Marie want to go to with me?
> Is there a group of girls I can go to the concert with so we can have fun and look after each other?

### Eventbrite

Eventbrite helps users discover and register for concrete events.

That is valuable when the user is already actively searching for something to do. The bad situations in this project often begin earlier: someone encounters an activity incidentally while using another platform and has only weak, tentative interest.

- Eventbrite:  
  https://www.eventbrite.com/

### Meetup

Meetup helps people find communities and organized activities around shared interests.

This works well when a user is willing to join a group or deliberately search for a community. WeShould targets a lighter state: someone may not want to join a pottery community, but may happily try pottery once if a friend also wants to go.

- Meetup:  
  https://www.meetup.com/

### Timeleft

Timeleft reduces coordination friction more directly by organizing social experiences and grouping participants.

Its approach shows the value of reducing the work required to make an offline experience happen. However, it primarily creates new social encounters around activities it organizes.

WeShould focuses instead on activities users already encounter and people they already know or could plausibly know/meet.

### Reservation and booking services

Services such as OpenTable, Resy, Eventbrite, and venue-specific ticketing systems are valuable at the final transactional stage.

WeShould should not attempt to replace them.

Once users decide to act on an intention, the application can send them to the relevant external booking or ticketing service.

## Solution Sketch

WeShould preserves weak intentions separately from concrete plans.

When a user sees a restaurant, class, concert, or activity on another app they can use the normal system Share action and choose WeShould.

The user is then asked for one lightweight decision:

> **I'd Go**

No date, guest list, event creation, or calendar comparison is required.

That intention persists.

If a connected friend later expresses interest in the same underlying activity, WeShould can reveal the overlap:

> **You + Marie would both go**  
> Wally's Jazz Cafe

Only then does scheduling begin.

Either person can choose **Make a Plan**, propose a time, and invite the matched participants to accept or decline. If everyone agrees, the activity becomes a committed plan.

If tickets, reservations, or registration are required, WeShould directs users to the existing external service rather than reproducing that functionality.

The core progression is:

```text
I saw it
  ↓
I'd go
  ↓
We both would
  ↓
We're going
```

## Stakeholders

### Activity seekers

People who encounter restaurants, classes, events, or experiences they would genuinely consider doing.

Their central problem is preserving that intention without immediately taking on the cost of planning.

### Friends and acquaintances

People whose interest can make an activity more likely to happen.

Their willingness is valuable even when they are not ready to make plans at the same moment.

### Activity providers

Restaurants, studios, museums, gyms, theaters, event organizers, and other organizations that provide the underlying experiences.

They benefit indirectly when latent interest becomes attendance but they do not need to adopt WeShould.

### Discovery platforms

TikTok, Instagram, websites, blogs, and other services on which users encounter activities.

WeShould treats these platforms as discovery sources rather than competitors. It should consume only content that users explicitly share through normal system mechanisms.

### Booking platforms

Services such as Resy, OpenTable, Eventbrite, and venue-specific ticketing systems handle transactions once users are ready to commit.

They remain external to WeShould.
