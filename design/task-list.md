## Task List

I conducted two usability studies with Jamie and Riley, using separate WeShould accounts with the same starting data. Each participant completed seven tasks covering the main workflow, from saving an activity to completing an outing. I asked them to think aloud as they worked and tried not to give them instructions about where to click. After each task, I also asked what they thought had happened, particularly when they had saved something or changed a plan's status.

## Findings

The main thing I noticed was that Jamie and Riley could generally figure out how to use the app, but weren't always sure what their actions meant afterward. I had been more worried about whether people would find the right buttons or understand how to move between screens. Instead, a lot of their questions were about whether something was private, whether their friends could see it, or whether a plan was actually happening.

Jamie tended to move through the tasks pretty quickly and was comfortable figuring things out as he went. Riley was more likely to stop and check what an action would do before moving on. I found that contrast helpful, especially when they approached the same feature differently.

## Interpretation and Next Iteration

Going into the studies, I was mostly concerned about whether people would understand how to move through WeShould. There are a few different concepts in the app, like saved activities, shared interests, proposals, and committed plans, and I wasn't sure how intuitive those distinctions would be to someone seeing it for the first time. What surprised me was that Jamie and Riley generally figured out what to do. Most of the uncertainty came afterward, when they wanted to know exactly what an action had changed.

The clearest example was the Gardner Museum proposal. Jamie submitted it and then asked whether it was already a plan or whether everyone still needed to accept. Riley also wanted to check its status. But in the next task, when they accepted invitations themselves, both understood that the outing had become confirmed. I think that's an important distinction. People seemed to understand the idea of a confirmed plan, but the app wasn't doing enough to show them that a proposal was still waiting on responses. That's the first thing I would fix, especially because misunderstanding whether a plan is confirmed could cause problems outside the app.

I also thought the difference between Jamie and Riley's approaches to scheduling was interesting. Jamie was happy to put "Friday evening" and deal with the details later. Riley wanted to know whether she was supposed to enter something more specific. I originally chose free-text scheduling because I didn't want making a plan in WeShould to feel like creating a calendar event. Part of the point of the app is that making plans with friends should feel relatively casual. I still like that decision, but I hadn't considered how someone who prefers more structure would use the same field. I think an optional date and time picker would be a good compromise, since it wouldn't take away the flexibility Jamie liked.

The questions about "I'd Go" also made me reconsider something I had taken for granted. To me, clicking "I'd Go" has always meant saving something you might want to do, not making a commitment. But Jamie immediately wanted to make sure he wasn't actually agreeing to go, and Riley wondered whether the action would tell her friends. Since saving interests is such a central part of WeShould, I don't want users having to think too hard about it. I would probably keep the label, because I think it fits the tone of the app, but add a short explanation of what clicking it actually does. I'd do something similar for connection requests so people know what information becomes visible.

The cancellation and completion tasks made me think more about feedback in general. I don't think I would change the underlying behavior. Cancelling a specific outing shouldn't remove the activity from someone's saved interests, and it makes sense for completed outings to move into history. But Jamie's hesitation about cancelling and his initial trouble finding the completed Wilson's Farm outing showed me that those transitions could be clearer. A simple message explaining what happened, or a link to the completed plan, would probably have prevented most of that confusion.

Overall, I don't think the studies pointed to a need for a major redesign of WeShould. Both participants could complete the workflow, and some of the features I was most concerned about, like finding shared interests, were understood without much explanation. What I'd focus on next is making the consequences of each action more obvious. I want users to be able to save something, suggest a plan, or accept an invitation without having to wonder what their friends can see or whether they've just committed to something.
