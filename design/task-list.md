
# P5 User Study: Task List

## Research Question

Can users distinguish between saving an activity they're interested in, discovering shared interests with friends, proposing an outing, and making a confirmed plan? Can they figure out how to move between these stages without being explicitly shown how the app works?


## Participants and Session Structure

We will conduct the study with two college students who have experience making social plans with friends. We hope to observe how each participant approaches the app, where they encounter confusion, and whether they interpret the different planning states as intended.

Each participant will use a separate test account with a similar set of friends, saved activities, and existing plans. This will let us test the same features across both sessions without one participant's actions affecting the other's experience.


Sessions will last approximately 60 minutes:

- **5 minutes:** Introduction, consent, and a brief explanation of the study.
- **35 minutes:** Participants work through seven tasks independently while thinking aloud.
- **20 minutes:** Follow-up questions about their experience, any confusing interactions, and their understanding of the app.

We will avoid explaining specific features before the tasks. If a participant gets stuck, we'll give them time to explore before offering help, and record when intervention was necessary. Task times are approximate rather than strict cutoffs.

## User Study Tasks

| Task | Participant-facing instruction | What we're evaluating |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Save an idea (4 min)** | "A friend sent you a link to Wally's Café jazz night. You're interested, but not ready to make plans. Keep it for later." | Whether users can save an activity and understand that marking "I'd Go" expresses interest rather than committing to attend. We'll also look at whether they can tell that the activity was successfully saved. |
| **2. Find common ground (5 min)** | "Find something you and a friend would both be interested in doing. What can you tell about your friend's interest from what you see?" | Whether users can discover overlapping interests and understand the difference between sharing an interest and actually having plans together. |
| **3. Respond to a connection (4 min)** | "A classmate you know has sent you a connection request. Find the request, decide whether to accept it, and check what happened." | Whether incoming requests are easy to find, whether the accept/decline options are understandable, and whether users recognize when a connection has been established. |
| **4. Coordinate a group (8 min)** | "You, Nora, and Theo are all interested in the same activity. Try to organize an outing for Friday evening with both of them. They haven't agreed to anything yet." | Whether users can find the relevant activity, choose the right people, set a time, and create a proposal. We also want to see whether they understand that sending a proposal doesn't mean everyone has agreed to attend. |
| **5. Respond to a proposal (5 min)** | "You've been invited to an outing on Saturday. Find the invitation, decide whether you can attend, and check the status of the plan afterward." | Whether users can locate and respond to invitations and understand how their response affects the plan. In the test setup, the other invitees have already accepted, so accepting should confirm the plan. We'll also observe whether users understand what happens if they decline. |
| **6. Change plans without losing interest (5 min)** | "Your Thursday outing is no longer possible, but you'd still like to do the activity another time. Make the necessary changes." | Whether users can cancel an existing plan without removing the activity from their saved interests, and whether the interface makes that distinction clear. |
| **7. Close the loop (4 min)** | "You and your friends already visited the museum. Update the outing to reflect that it happened, then see what happened to the original activity." | Whether users can mark a plan as completed, understand what happens to the original saved interest, and find the outing afterward. |

## Study Setup

The tasks are arranged to cover the main stages of planning an outing, from expressing initial interest to completing a plan. The first three tasks focus on saving activities, finding shared interests, and connecting with other users. The remaining tasks test how users create, respond to, change, and complete plans.

We will prepare the accounts with a small set of realistic activities, connections, and plans in different states. Tasks 4–7 will use separate activities and plans so that getting stuck on one task does not prevent a participant from attempting the next one.

For Task 5, the other invitees will already have accepted the proposal. This allows us to observe whether the participant recognizes the change from a pending proposal to a confirmed plan if they choose to accept.

We'll keep the starting conditions consistent across participants, while allowing them to navigate and make decisions on their own.

## Evaluation

For each task, we'll record whether participants complete it independently, how long it takes, and any difficulties they encounter. We'll pay particular attention to moments when users hesitate, take an unexpected path, misunderstand a feature, or recover from a mistake without assistance.

A task will count as independently completed if the participant reaches the intended outcome without being told which actions to take. For tasks involving a choice, such as accepting or declining a request, we'll evaluate whether the participant can carry out their chosen action and correctly understand the result.

We'll also ask participants to explain what they think happened after completing key actions. This is important because a user might successfully click through a task without understanding whether they've saved an interest, sent an invitation, or confirmed a plan.

During the debrief, we'll ask which parts of the process felt intuitive, which distinctions were confusing, and whether participants would feel confident using the app to organize a real outing with friends. We'll compare observations across sessions to identify recurring usability issues and prioritize changes to the interface.
