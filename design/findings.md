## Interpretation and Next Iteration

The biggest thing I took away from these studies was that people could generally figure out how to use WeShould, but they weren't always sure what their actions meant afterward. I had been more concerned about whether users would understand how to navigate between saved activities, shared interests, and plans. Instead, a lot of the uncertainty was about what was being shared or whether something had actually been confirmed.

The Gardner Museum task was probably the clearest example. Jamie submitted the proposal and immediately wanted to know whether it was already a plan or whether everyone else still needed to accept. Riley also checked whether it was pending. But in the next task, when they accepted invitations themselves, both understood that the outing had become committed. That made me think I don't necessarily need to change how proposing and confirming work. I need to make it much clearer when a plan is still waiting on other people. This would be my first priority because I don't want someone treating an outing as confirmed when their friends haven't agreed to it.

I also realized that I was taking the meaning of "I'd Go" for granted. To me, it obviously meant saving something you might want to do later. But Jamie wanted to make sure he wasn't actually agreeing to go, and Riley wondered whether her friends would be notified. The questions about connection requests were similar. The participants understood why they might want to connect with someone, but weren't entirely sure what that person would be able to see. Since the whole idea behind WeShould is to make expressing interest less awkward than directly asking someone to make plans, I think these details matter. If saving something makes users worry about what they're committing to or who they're notifying, it isn't really accomplishing what I intended.

The scheduling task was interesting for a different reason. Jamie was perfectly happy proposing "Friday evening" and figuring out the details later, while Riley wanted to know whether the time needed to be more specific. I originally chose free-text scheduling because I didn't want suggesting an outing to feel like filling out a calendar event. I still think that flexibility is valuable, but Riley's reaction made me realize that some people would appreciate more guidance. I would probably add an optional date and time picker rather than require everyone to schedule the same way.

The cancellation and completion tasks also made me think about how the app handles changes in status. I don't think I would change the underlying behavior. Cancelling a Thursday outing shouldn't mean losing interest in Wilson's Farm entirely, and completed plans make sense in history rather than alongside upcoming ones. But Jamie wasn't sure whether cancelling would remove the activity, and he had to look around before finding the completed outing. Even though he eventually found it, I think a simple confirmation message or a direct link to history would have made the experience better.

Overall, I was encouraged that both participants could get through the workflow and understood important parts of the app, especially finding shared interests and recognizing a confirmed invitation. I don't think I need to rethink the main structure of WeShould based on these sessions. For the next iteration, I'd focus on making the results of actions clearer: what gets saved, what other people can see, when a proposal is still pending, and where plans go after they're cancelled or completed. These are relatively small changes, but I think they'd make the app feel much more predictable without taking away the casual approach I wanted in the first place.
